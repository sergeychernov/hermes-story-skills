# Live-event venue dining and arrival planning

Use this reference when a traveller asks whether to eat before a concert, jazz club, theatre, festival, or other timed event during an active travel story.

## Verification order

1. **Identify the exact event before researching the venue.** Match artist/title, local date, start time, city, and venue. Prefer the venue's current official calendar. Search results for the same artist often surface old or different-city events; reject any page whose date or venue does not match.
2. **Read the venue's arrival rules.** Check the official event page and any “before you come”, FAQ, reservation, or house-rules page for:
   - door-opening and start/end times;
   - reservation hold deadline;
   - assigned, arrival-order, shared, or standing seating;
   - set/intermission structure;
   - ticket price and whether any food/drink credit is included.
3. **Separate four different food claims:**
   - drinks/bar service exists;
   - snacks exist;
   - full meal categories exist;
   - the kitchen will actually be accepting orders at the relevant time.
   Never infer full dinner from “bar”, and never infer late kitchen hours from a photographed menu.
4. **Verify the menu with source labels.** Prefer a current first-party menu. If the official site has no menu, a recent, legible, date-labelled user menu photo may establish the visible categories and approximate prices, but report it as third-party evidence. It does not establish current stock, service hours, or inclusion in the ticket.
5. **Make the recommendation around time pressure, not menu existence alone.**
   - Recommend dinner beforehand when the venue is primarily performance-led, kitchen hours are unknown, seating is arrival-order/shared, or eating during the set would undermine the experience.
   - If a current menu clearly offers full meals and there is enough time between doors and show, say that eating there is viable, but advise arriving at doors and ordering immediately.
   - Distinguish “you can eat there” from “it is the better place for a relaxed dinner”.

## Compact response pattern

Lead with one decisive sentence:

> Food and drinks are available, but for a relaxed full dinner I would eat beforehand; if you prefer to eat at the venue, arrive when doors open and order immediately.

Then give only the operational facts:

- doors / show / end;
- reservation deadline and seating rule;
- verified food scope (snacks vs salads/pasta/mains);
- source caveat for menu recency and unknown kitchen closing time.

Do not drown a same-day traveller in a long source audit. State uncertainty exactly where it affects the decision.

## Proven research pattern: Nardis Jazz Club

The official Nardis calendar established the exact 25 July 2026 event, 21:30–00:00, and ticket price. Its official **Gelmeden Önce** page established doors at 20:30, reservation hold until 21:15, arrival-order seating, limited two-person tables, shared tables, two live sets, and no standing audience. The official pages did not state kitchen hours.

A Restaurant Guru page exposed a legible menu image labelled as user-added three months earlier. Visual inspection showed drinks plus snacks, wraps, salads, pasta, and main courses. That supported “full food exists”, but not “the kitchen is guaranteed open through the concert”. The correct recommendation therefore separated viability from preference: eating there was possible if arriving at doors and ordering immediately; a relaxed dinner was safer beforehand.
